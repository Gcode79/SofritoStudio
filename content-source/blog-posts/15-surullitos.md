If you've ever walked through a kiosk in Piñones or a beach vendor in Piña Colada territory, you've seen **surullitos** (soo-roo-YOH-tohs). Golden, crispy, torpedo-shaped corn sticks, deep-fried until crunchy on the outside and soft-sweet on the inside. They're Puerto Rican comfort food in its purest form — corn, sugar, and heat.

My abuela called them "surullitos de maíz" — corn surullitos — because the word "surullito" doesn't really translate. It's one of those Puerto Rican words that only exists on the island. Some people think it comes from "surullar" (to roll), because of the shape. Others say it's just one of those boricua words that means what it means. Either way, once you eat one, you don't need a translation.

The key ingredient is **masa de maíz** — the same fresh corn dough used for pasteles. But here's the mainland hack: canned cream-style corn works beautifully. Abuela would raise an eyebrow, but she'd also admit that the results are pretty close. The natural sweetness of the corn does most of the work; you just add a little sugar, a little salt, and a whole lot of hot oil.

Surullitos are part of the Comida Callejera (street food) tradition in Puerto Rico. You'll find them at kiosks, beachside stands, and roadside vendors across the island. They're usually served with **mojo isleño** — a garlicky, vinegary dipping sauce that cuts through the sweetness. Some places serve them with mayo-ketchup. Both work. But the classic is mojo.

This **surullitos recipe** makes about 20 sticks — enough for a party appetizer or a family snack. The batter comes together in 5 minutes. The frying takes another 10. Total time: under 20 minutes. It's one of the fastest Puerto Rican recipes you can make.

The secret to crispy surullitos? **Drain the corn well** (if using canned), and **don't make the batter too wet.** If the batter is too thin, the surullitos will spread in the oil and lose their shape. You want a thick, scoopable batter that holds its form.

Let's make surullitos. *Vamos a hacer surullitos.*

### Ingredients

- 1 can (14.75 oz) cream-style corn
- 1 can (15.25 oz) whole kernel corn, drained well
- 1 cup masa harina (corn flour, like Maseca)
- ¼ cup granulated sugar
- ½ teaspoon salt
- ½ teaspoon garlic powder
- 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour (if batter is too thin)
- Vegetable oil for frying (about 2 inches deep)

**For mojo isleño dipping sauce:**
- ½ cup olive oil
- 4 cloves garlic, minced
- ¼ cup white vinegar
- 1 tablespoon fresh lime juice
- ½ teaspoon dried oregano
- Salt to taste

---

### Instructions

**Make the batter:**

1. **Combine the corn.** In a large bowl, mix the cream-style corn and the drained whole kernel corn. You want some texture from the whole kernels, so don't over-blend.

2. **Add dry ingredients.** Stir in the masa harina, sugar, salt, and garlic powder. Mix until a thick batter forms. It should hold its shape when scooped — like thick cookie dough. If it's too wet, add flour 1 tablespoon at a time. If too dry, add a splash of the cream corn liquid. *(Abuela's tip: "The batter should hold a shape when you drop it. If it spreads, it's too thin.")*

3. **Rest for 5 minutes.** Let the batter sit while you heat the oil. The masa harina absorbs moisture and the batter will firm up slightly.

**Make the mojo:**

4. **Heat the oil.** In a small saucepan, heat the olive oil over medium-low heat. Add the minced garlic and cook for 1–2 minutes until fragrant but not browned.

5. **Finish the mojo.** Remove from heat. Stir in the vinegar, lime juice, oregano, and salt. Transfer to a small serving bowl. Let cool to room temperature.

**Fry the surullitos:**

6. **Heat frying oil.** In a cast iron skillet or deep pot, heat 2 inches of vegetable oil to 350°F (175°C).

7. **Shape and fry.** Using a spoon or your hands, shape the batter into small torpedo-shaped sticks, about 3 inches long and 1 inch thick. Carefully lower them into the hot oil. Fry 4–5 at a time — don't crowd the pan.

8. **Fry until golden.** Cook for 2–3 minutes per side, turning occasionally, until deep golden brown and crispy all over. The inside should be soft and slightly sweet.

9. **Drain and serve.** Transfer to a wire rack over paper towels. Sprinkle with a tiny pinch of salt while hot. Serve immediately with mojo isleño.

---

### Chef's Notes & Abuela Tips

- **"El maíz tiene que estar escurrido"** — Drain the whole kernel corn well. Excess liquid makes the batter too thin and the surullitos will spread in the oil.
- **Fresh corn version:** If you have fresh corn on the cob, cut the kernels off and blend half of them into a rough puree. Use the other half whole. This gives you the best of both worlds — creaminess and texture.
- **Masa harina is key.** Don't substitute cornmeal — it's gritty and won't bind. Masa harina (like Maseca brand) is the fine corn flour used for tortillas and tamales. Find it in the international aisle or at any Latin grocery store.
- **Don't make them too thick.** If the surullitos are too thick, the outside burns before the inside cooks. Keep them about 1 inch in diameter for even cooking.
- **Serve hot.** Surullitos lose their crunch within 15 minutes. Make them last — fry them just before serving.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I bake surullitos instead of frying?**
You can, but the texture changes dramatically — they'll be softer and less crispy. If you want to try, preheat oven to 400°F, place shaped surullitos on a parchment-lined baking sheet sprayed with cooking oil, and bake for 15–20 minutes, flipping halfway. They'll be more like cornbread sticks than the crispy street-food version.

**What is masa harina and where do I find it?**
Masa harina is finely ground corn flour treated with lime (nixtamalized). It's used for tortillas, tamales, and — in this recipe — surullitos. Look for Maseca brand in the international/Latin aisle of most grocery stores, or at any Latin market. Don't confuse it with cornmeal (like Jiffy) — cornmeal is grittier and won't work the same way.

**Can I use fresh corn instead of canned?**
Yes, and it's arguably better. Use 4 ears of fresh corn — cut the kernels off, blend half into a rough puree, and leave the other half whole. The fresh corn has more natural sweetness and a brighter flavor. Adjust sugar down to 2 tablespoons if using fresh corn.

**What else can I dip surullitos in?**
Mojo isleño is traditional, but mayo-ketchup (salsa rosada) is popular too. Some people eat them plain — they're sweet enough on their own. For a spicy twist, add a pinch of cayenne to the mojo. See more in our [Comida Callejera](/blog.html#comida-callejera) collection.
