Asopao de Pollo

Thicker than soup, wetter than arroz con pollo — Puerto Rican chicken and rice stew with olives and capers.

1 hour Medium

By Josh Ortiz·August 2026·6 min read

Asopao is the hybrid that Puerto Rico does better than anyone — thicker than soup, wetter than arroz con pollo, and loaded with chicken, olives, and capers. It sits right in the middle of the Puerto Rican comfort food spectrum: a stew that's saucy enough to eat with a spoon but hearty enough to fill you up completely. The rice cooks directly in the broth, absorbing the tomato-saffron liquid and releasing starch that thickens everything into a glossy, stew-like consistency. Add briny olives and salty capers, and you've got a dish that straddles the line between comfort and celebration. Asopao is what you make when arroz con pollo feels too dry and sancocho feels too ambitious. It's the one-pot answer to a weeknight dinner that tastes like it took all day.

What is Asopao?

Asopao is Puerto Rico's beloved chicken and rice stew — a dish that exists in the space between soup and rice. The word comes from "asopar," which means to eat soup, but asopao is much more substantial than any broth with noodles. It's rice cooked in a tomato-based broth with chicken, olives, capers, and sofrito, simmered uncovered so the rice releases its starch and thickens the liquid into a rich, stew-like consistency. The result is something that's eaten with a spoon but satisfies like a full plate dinner.

Every Puerto Rican family makes asopao slightly differently. Some add peas, some add diced ham, some use shellfish instead of or in addition to chicken. But the core identity is always the same: rice, chicken, tomato broth, olives, and capers. It's the dish that bridges the gap between everyday cooking and special occasions — easy enough for a Tuesday night, impressive enough for a Sunday gathering. In Puerto Rico, asopao is what you bring to someone who's feeling under the weather, what you cook when you want to impress without overthinking, and what you crave when nothing else will do.

Ingredients

Scale recipe:
6 servings

For 6 servings:

  • 2 lbs chicken thighs — bone-in, skin-on for the best flavor. Boneless works too, but the bone adds depth to the broth.
  • 1.5 cups rice — long-grain white rice. Don't use instant — it'll turn to mush before the flavors develop.
  • 1/4 cup sofrito — the aromatic base. Homemade is best, but Goya frozen works in a pinch.
  • 1 packet sazon — sazon con culantro y achiote for color and the signature orange hue.
  • 1 cup tomato sauce — gives the broth its tomato base and body.
  • 6 cups chicken broth — low-sodium so you can control the salt level.
  • 1/2 cup manzanilla olives — the briny pops that cut through the richness of the stew.
  • 2 tablespoons capers — salty, tangy, and essential. Don't skip them.
  • 2 tablespoons oil — vegetable or olive oil for browning the chicken.
  • Salt and pepper to taste

Mainland Swaps

  • No sazon? Mix 1 tsp turmeric + 1/2 tsp garlic powder + 1/4 tsp cumin. It won't be exactly the same, but it gives you the color and base flavor.
  • No capers? Use a small amount of chopped green olives as a substitute. The briny, salty quality is what matters.
  • Want seafood asopao? Swap the chicken for shrimp and add them in the last 5 minutes of cooking. Shrimp asopao is a beloved variation.

Step-by-Step Instructions

1
Brown the chicken. Season the chicken thighs with salt and pepper. Heat oil in a large, heavy pot over medium-high heat. Brown the chicken on both sides — about 4 minutes per side. Remove and set aside.
2
Saute the sofrito. In the same pot with the chicken fat, add the sofrito and cook for 2-3 minutes until fragrant. Scrape up any browned bits from the bottom — that's pure flavor.
3
Add sazon and tomato sauce. Stir in the sazon packet and tomato sauce. Cook for 2 minutes, stirring to combine. The achiote in the sazon will turn everything a beautiful orange-red.
4
Toast the rice. Add the rice and stir for 1 minute to coat every grain in the sofrito-tomato mixture. This toasting step helps the rice hold its shape during the long simmer.
5
Add broth, chicken, olives, and capers. Pour in the chicken broth, nestle the browned chicken thighs back in, and add the olives and capers. Bring to a rolling boil.
6
Simmer uncovered 30 minutes. Reduce heat to medium-low and simmer without a lid, stirring occasionally to prevent sticking. The rice will release starch and thicken the broth into a stew-like consistency. It's done when the rice is tender and the consistency is thick but still spoonable.

Tips for Perfect Asopao

  • Simmer uncovered. The lid stays off for the entire 30-minute simmer. This lets the rice release starch and thicken the broth naturally. Covering it would make the rice fluffy instead of stewy.
  • Stir occasionally. Unlike arroz con pollo where you don't touch the rice, asopao needs occasional stirring. The starch settling on the bottom can stick and burn if you ignore it completely.
  • Use chicken thighs. Breast meat dries out during the long simmer. Thighs stay juicy and tender, and the dark meat adds richness to the broth.
  • Don't overcook the rice. Asopao should be thick but the rice should still have texture. Start checking at 25 minutes — overcooked rice turns the stew into porridge.

Frequently Asked Questions

The key difference is liquid ratio and cooking method. Arroz con pollo is cooked covered with less liquid, producing fluffy, separate rice grains. Asopao uses more liquid and is cooked uncovered, so the rice releases starch and thickens the broth into a stew-like consistency. Think of asopao as arroz con pollo's saucier, more comforting cousin.
Long-grain white rice is the standard. It holds its shape during the long simmer while still releasing enough starch to thicken the broth. Jasmine rice works too. Avoid instant rice — it breaks down too fast and turns the stew gummy. Short-grain or arborio will make it too thick and starchy.
Shrimp asopao is a beloved Puerto Rican variation. Add 1 lb of large, peeled shrimp during the last 5 minutes of cooking so they don't overcook. You can also add crab meat or chunks of firm white fish. Some families make a mixed version with both chicken and shrimp — the combination of land and sea is incredible.
