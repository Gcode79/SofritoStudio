Chocolate Caliente Boricua

Puerto Rican hot chocolate — thick, cinnamon-spiced, and made with real chocolate. Not the packet stuff.

15 minutes Easy

By Josh Ortiz·August 2026·3 min read

Chocolate caliente boricua is Puerto Rican hot chocolate — and it's nothing like the watered-down packet stuff you might be used to. This is the real thing: thick, rich, cinnamon-spiced, and made with actual chocolate melted into whole milk until it's practically a dessert in a mug. In Puerto Rico, chocolate caliente is what you drink on Christmas Eve after the piñata, what abuela makes when someone has a cold, and what warms you up on a cool mountain evening in Adjuntas or Jayuya. The chocolate melts into the milk as it heats, creating a drink that's somewhere between hot cocoa and liquid ganache. Add a cinnamon stick for stirring, and you've got the kind of drink that makes you close your eyes on the first sip and forget about everything else.

What is Chocolate Caliente Boricua?

Chocolate caliente boricua is Puerto Rico's version of hot chocolate — made with real chocolate (not cocoa powder), whole milk, sugar, and cinnamon. The traditional Puerto Rican approach uses Mexican chocolate tablets like Ibarra or Abuelita, which come pre-mixed with sugar and cinnamon. These tablets dissolve into hot milk and create a drink that's thick, fragrant, and deeply satisfying. If you can't find Mexican chocolate, a combination of good dark chocolate and ground cinnamon works almost as well.

What sets Puerto Rican hot chocolate apart from the American version is the richness. It's made with real chocolate melted into milk, not powder mixed into water. The result is a drink that's closer to liquid dessert than a light beverage. Some families add a pinch of chili powder for heat, some whisk it until it's frothy with a wooden molinillo, and some serve it with mallorcas or quesitos for dipping. But the core — real chocolate, real milk, real cinnamon — stays the same. In Puerto Rico, chocolate caliente isn't a seasonal treat. It's a year-round comfort that just happens to be extra popular during the holidays.

Ingredients

Scale recipe:
4 servings

For 4 servings:

  • 4 oz Mexican chocolate — Ibarra or Abuelita tablets, which come with sugar and cinnamon already mixed in. If you can't find them, use 4 oz dark chocolate (70% cacao) + 1/2 tsp ground cinnamon.
  • 4 cups whole milk — the full-fat richness is what makes this drink special. 2% works in a pinch, but don't use skim.
  • 1/4 cup sugar — adjust to taste. Mexican chocolate is already sweetened, so start with less if using Ibarra or Abuelita.
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract — rounds out the chocolate flavor and adds warmth.
  • Pinch of salt — enhances the chocolate flavor and balances the sweetness.
  • Cinnamon sticks for serving — one per mug. Use them to stir as you drink — the cinnamon infuses the chocolate as it sits.

Mainland Swaps

  • No Mexican chocolate? Use 4 oz of good dark chocolate (60-70% cacao) + 1/2 tsp ground cinnamon + an extra tablespoon of sugar. It won't have the exact same texture, but the flavor is close.
  • No whole milk? Oat milk is the best dairy-free substitute — it's creamy and neutral-flavored. Almond milk works but makes a thinner drink. Coconut milk makes it richer but adds coconut flavor.
  • Want it thicker? Add 1 tablespoon of cornstarch mixed with a splash of cold milk to the warming milk. Whisk constantly as it thickens.

Step-by-Step Instructions

1
Heat the milk. Pour the milk into a medium saucepan and heat over medium heat until steaming and small bubbles form around the edges. Don't let it boil — boiling can scald the milk and change the texture.
2
Melt the chocolate. Break the Mexican chocolate tablet into pieces and add to the hot milk. Stir continuously with a whisk or wooden spoon until the chocolate is completely melted and the milk turns a rich, deep brown.
3
Sweeten and flavor. Add the sugar, vanilla, and pinch of salt. Whisk vigorously for about a minute — this incorporates air and creates the frothy top that Puerto Ricans love. Taste and adjust sugar if needed.
4
Serve with a cinnamon stick. Pour into mugs — the frothy top should be thick and inviting. Drop a cinnamon stick into each mug. Stir with the stick as you drink; it keeps infusing the chocolate with cinnamon warmth.

Tips for Perfect Chocolate Caliente

  • Don't boil the milk. Scalded milk has a skin on top and an off-flavor. Heat it until it's steaming and just barely simmering — small bubbles at the edges, not a rolling boil.
  • Whisk vigorously. The frothy top is a signature of Puerto Rican hot chocolate. Whisk hard for the last minute of cooking to incorporate air. If you have a molinillo (wooden whisk), roll it between your palms for the traditional frothing technique.
  • Use real chocolate. Cocoa powder mixed with water or milk makes hot cocoa, not chocolate caliente. The real thing uses actual melted chocolate, which gives it the richness and body that defines the drink.
  • Serve immediately. Chocolate caliente is best fresh and hot. The froth dissipates as it cools, and the chocolate can thicken as it sits. Make it right before you're ready to drink it.
