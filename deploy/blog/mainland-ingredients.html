Where to Find Puerto Rican Ingredients on the Mainland

Recao, ají dulce, sofrito, gandules, sazón — where to buy them, what to ask for, and the easy mainland swaps that keep your cocina boricua.

8 min read Mainland-tested

By Josh Ortiz·August 2026·8 min read

When I moved off the island, the hardest part wasn't the recipes — it was the ingredients. I'd walk into a mainland supermarket looking for recao and come out empty-handed. The good news: almost everything you need for authentic Puerto Rican cooking is available in the U.S. — you just have to know where to look and what to ask for. This guide covers every staple in the Ortiz kitchen, the stores that carry it, and the swap that works when you can't find it. Once your pantry is stocked, cooking traditional island flavors at home becomes as simple as cooking anywhere.

The Short Version

Start with three stops, and you'll have 90% of what you need:

  • Your local Latin grocery store or bodega — recao, sofrito, sazón, adobo, plantains, pigeon peas, and most canned goods.
  • An Asian or Indian market — fresh culantro (the same plant as recao), sold as sawtooth coriander, plus yuca and root vegetables.
  • Your regular supermarket's international aisle — the basics like adobo, sazón, and frozen plantains are more common than you'd think.

Where to Shop, Neighborhood by Neighborhood

Every major mainland city with a Caribbean community has a reliable source. A few patterns that hold everywhere:

  • New York, Orlando, Miami, Chicago, Houston — dedicated Puerto Rican and Dominican bodegas carry everything, including fresh recao and frozen ají dulce year-round.
  • Mexican-focused Latin markets — excellent for culantro, plantains, yuca, and guava paste. Just ask for "culantro" — they'll know it.
  • Asian and Indian grocers — the secret weapon for fresh recao (sawtooth coriander), Thai basil, and malanga.
  • Online — frozen sofrito, recao, and ají dulce ship nationwide from specialty retailers when you have no local option.
  • Your own garden — recao and ají dulce are surprisingly hardy. A single culantro plant on a sunny windowsill out-produces any store.

The Mainland Swaps Cheat Sheet

  • Recao missing? Use double cilantro, or fresh sawtooth coriander from the Asian market.
  • Ají dulce missing? Cubanelle pepper + a whisper of seedless Scotch bonnet.
  • No fresh sofrito? Jarred or frozen recaito from the Latin aisle — or make a 20-minute batch and freeze it in cubes.
  • No sazón? Mix paprika, garlic powder, cumin, and oregano — that's what's inside the envelope anyway.
  • No gandules? Canned pigeon peas are the honest substitute; black beans are the easy one.

Every recipe in our cookbooks lists these swaps right on the card — so you're never stuck, whether you're a diaspora cook in a city with three bodegas or in a town with none.

Frequently Asked Questions

Recao is the Puerto Rican name for culantro (Eryngium foetidum). At Latin grocery stores it's sold as culantro or recao in the produce section. At Asian and Indian markets it's sold as sawtooth coriander or ngo gai. Frozen recao is the easiest option nationwide.
Yes, as a last resort. Cilantro is milder than recao, so double the amount — use 1 cup of cilantro where the recipe calls for a bunch of recao. The flavor will be less pungent but still works in most Puerto Rican dishes.
Ají dulce is sold frozen or jarred at most Latin grocery stores. It's a small, sweet, aromatic pepper that's hard to find fresh off the island. If you can't find it, a mix of cubanelle pepper and a pinch of Scotch bonnet (seedless) gets you close.
Sazón is a seasoned salt blend used to color and flavor rice, stews, and meats — it usually contains achiote (annatto), which gives it that orange color. Adobo is a simpler all-purpose seasoning of garlic, oregano, salt, and pepper used as a rub. Both are in the Latin foods aisle of most supermarkets.
